# MacDuo design

MacDuo prepares one screenshot of the built-in display at 92°, before the visible effect starts below 90°. It projects that image through the moving physical screen onto a virtual desktop fixed upright at the hinge. Closing and reopening use the same image and the same angle-derived projection after entry. Opening to 90° restores the normal desktop; reaching 94° discards the screenshot and rearms capture. The two-degree preparation/reset band prevents repeated captures from quantized sensor jitter.

At the default 100% perspective strength, the picture follows one assumed eye position, adjustable in the preview. The default is straight on: 60 cm in front of the hinge and vertically centered on the upright screen. Eye height is half the detected physical display height. Reset to straight-on restores that centered view; saved custom viewpoints remain available. During the first 15° of closing, the projection starts with 30% less stretch and smoothly reaches full physical correction at 75°. The deeper 60° and 45° projections remain fully corrected. Lower strengths blend the physical correction toward an unchanged image, weakening the upright illusion; the hinge remains fixed. No camera is needed. A Gaussian coverage mask softens the projected image boundary both inward and outward into black. At zero frost, the boundary remains sharp. Pixels outside the physical panel cannot be drawn.

The visible top boundary retreats from the bezel as the lid closes: 0% of panel height at 90°, 12% at 60°, and 22% at 45°. This aperture adds a depth cue while leaving the source projection unchanged and the bottom anchored at the hinge. Its boundary shares the Gaussian feathering. Frost follows the visible image height from hinge to top aperture. The bottom stays clear, the middle softens gradually, and the strongest diffusion sits at the top. Blur, desaturation, and tint share that vertical gradient, so a full-screen haze does not obscure the lower desktop. The upper image also darkens progressively as the lid closes. The final fade runs from 12° above the calibrated grazing angle to 2° above it, finishing before the panel becomes edge-on.

The main actor owns the screenshot request, window, and Metal rendering. The graphics pipeline warms at launch. At 92°, a precomputed Gaussian blur pyramid and an identity drawable are prepared while the overlay stays hidden. GPU completion gates presentation so no empty black first frame is exposed. Entry starts at zero opacity and fades in over 100 ms, while geometry follows the current filtered lid angle immediately. This avoids rendering an outdated angle during a fast close or late screenshot. A cadence-aware motion estimator fills the display frames between quantized angle reports. The sensor can return the same cached angle across several polls, so unchanged reports establish liveness without restarting the tween. Changed reports update the motion estimate, and a continuous curve reconciles them with the currently rendered position and velocity. Prediction is bounded and settles back to the held reading when movement stops. A CVDisplayLink tied to the built-in screen drives animation at its refresh rate, up to 120 Hz on ProMotion displays, while the overlay is visible. A locked bridge keeps only the latest timestamp and allows one pending main-thread draw, preventing stale frames from accumulating. Each callback predicts the angle for its presentation deadline and explicitly draws one Metal frame. Sensor and housekeeping callbacks only control eligibility; they never trigger extra draws. The blur pyramid stays unchanged throughout the gesture. Hiding, pausing, or suspending the effect invalidates the display link. Raw angles still control screenshot capture and immediate removal at 90°. A dedicated serial queue reads the HID sensor. A complete screenshot, valid recent angle, built-in screen, and enabled state are required before an overlay is shown. SCScreenshotManager excludes all windows of the app from the snapshot. There is no SCStream instance, recurring capture, recording output, audio capture, or network code. macOS still requires its Screen Recording permission for a screenshot.

One generation identifies each screenshot attempt. Moving back to 94°, pause, lock, sleep, sensor failure, and display reconfiguration synchronously hide the overlay, discard the image, and invalidate pending work. At 90° the overlay hides immediately and resets its entry clock; it retains the prepared image until 94°. A late screenshot cannot show an overlay above 90° or revive a canceled gesture. A failed attempt waits for a new gesture instead of retrying repeatedly. Distinct sleep, screen-sleep, session, and lock reasons prevent a screen-wake notification from resuming while still locked.

Coordinates use the hinge as origin, x right, y up, and z toward the viewer. Normalize lengths by physical screen height. A panel point at horizontal x and height v is P=(x,v sin(theta),v cos(theta)). The assumed eye is E=(0,h,d). Intersect the eye-to-panel ray with z=0 to find the desktop sample. At 90° this is identity; the hinge stays fixed. The view is edge-on at atan(h/d), so the image fades before that angle.

The design combines a single-screenshot capture path with a finite-eye Metal projection. A frozen image reduces capture work and matches the requested behavior. Simple layer rotation was rejected because it tilts the image along with the physical lid. Orthographic compensation was also considered; a calibrated eye point gives a closer physical model. The shader compiles at runtime, so full Xcode and an offline Metal compiler are unnecessary.

The overlay changes appearance only. Mouse events pass through at their original coordinates, and the system cursor stays available. The effect covers the menu bar and Dock. Opening to 90° restores them immediately; Control–Option–Command–L pauses the effect. Only the built-in display is affected. Normal near-closed blanking, lock, and sleep still apply. The first version uses SDR screenshots; protected video can appear black.

Verification uses actual sensor readings, an independent forward ray/plane oracle, offscreen GPU pixel checks, rendered previews, and screenshot lifecycle diagnostics. Preview and desktop overlay use the same renderer. The controls use a generated sample desktop, so calibration is available before screenshot permission is granted.
